# PART 3-A · RUMINANT DIGESTIVE PHYSIOLOGY (25 min, S3–S10)

## S3 · The four compartments
**SLIDE:**
| Compartment | Approx. capacity (adult dairy cow) | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Rumen** | **100–150 L** | Microbial fermentation vat; VFA absorption via papillae |
| **Reticulum** | 10–20 L | Mixing, hardware trap, contraction pacemaker; **where the bolus sits** |
| **Omasum** | 10–20 L | Water and VFA absorption, particle size filter |
| **Abomasum** | 15–20 L | **True stomach** — HCl, pepsin, pH 2–3 |
> The rumen and reticulum function as **one organ (reticulorumen)** and contract together **1–3 times per minute**.

**SAY:**
"Four compartments. Only the last one, the abomasum, is a true stomach in the sense that a human or a pig has one — acid and enzymes.

The first two, the rumen and reticulum, work as a single organ and contract one to three times a minute. Those contractions do two things: they mix the contents so the microbes can work, and they push gas up towards the oesophagus for eructation — for belching.

Remember that number, one to three contractions per minute, because when a cow is sick, rumen motility is one of the first things you can assess with your hand and your ear, and it is one of the first things to fail.

And note where the reticulum is — that is where a bolus sensor sits, for the rest of the animal's life."

**⏱ 4'**

## S4 · The cow does not digest feed — her microbes do
**SLIDE:**
| Microbe group | Substrate | Optimum pH | Products |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cellulolytic bacteria** (fibre digesters) | Cellulose, hemicellulose | **6.2–6.8 — sensitive to low pH** | Acetate, some butyrate |
| **Amylolytic bacteria** (starch digesters) | Starch, sugars | Tolerate **5.5–6.0** | Propionate, lactate |
| Protozoa | Starch, bacteria | Sensitive to low pH | Buffering effect (engulf starch) |
| Fungi | Lignified fibre | Neutral | Fibre breakdown |
| **Methanogenic archaea** | H₂ + CO₂ | Neutral | **Methane** |
> **This asymmetry is the origin of acidosis: when pH falls, the fibre digesters die first and the starch digesters thrive.**

**SAY:**
"Here is the single most important idea in ruminant nutrition. **The cow does not digest her feed. Her microbes do, and the cow digests the microbes and their products.**

Now look carefully at the pH column, because that column explains acidosis, laminitis, milk fat depression, and a large part of your infertility.

The fibre-digesting bacteria need a pH of about six point two to six point eight. They are fragile. The starch-digesting bacteria tolerate five point five, and some tolerate lower.

So what happens when you feed a lot of grain? The pH falls. The starch digesters do fine. The fibre digesters die. And now the cow cannot digest fibre — which is most of her ration in a tropical smallholder system.

**That asymmetry is the mechanism of acidosis.** Everything in the next section follows from this one slide."

**BOARD:** Draw a pH line from 5.0 to 7.0. Mark fibre digesters 6.2–6.8, starch digesters 5.5–6.0, SARA below 5.6.

**⏱ 6'**

## S5 · Volatile fatty acids — where milk actually comes from
**SLIDE:**
| VFA | Proportion of total | Made from | What the cow uses it for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Acetate** | **~60–70%** | **Fibre** | **Milk FAT** precursor; body energy |
| **Propionate** | ~15–20% | **Starch** | **Gluconeogenesis → GLUCOSE → milk LACTOSE → milk VOLUME** |
| **Butyrate** | ~10–15% | Both | Energy for the **rumen epithelium**; converted to **BHB** (a ketone body) in the rumen wall |
> VFA supply **60–75% of the cow's total energy**. She absorbs them across the rumen papillae.
> **The cow is not a sugar-eating animal. She is a VFA-powered animal.**

**SAY:**
"When the microbes ferment feed, they produce volatile fatty acids. These are the cow's fuel. Sixty to seventy-five percent of all her energy comes from VFAs absorbed directly through the rumen wall.

Three of them matter.

Acetate — sixty to seventy percent of the total — comes mainly from fibre and is the precursor of milk fat. **No fibre, no acetate, no butterfat.** That is why acidosis causes milk fat depression, and why the fat-to-protein ratio in milk is a diagnostic tool.

Propionate comes mainly from starch and is converted in the liver into glucose. Glucose becomes lactose, and lactose draws water into the udder, which determines milk **volume**. Remember this one — it comes back when we discuss ketosis, because the liver has to choose what to do with its oxaloacetate.

Butyrate feeds the rumen wall itself, and — this is important — the rumen wall converts some of it into beta-hydroxybutyrate. BHB. The same molecule we measure to diagnose ketosis.

So write this down: **the cow is not a sugar-eating animal. She is a VFA-powered animal.** If you feed her like a pig, you will make her sick."

**⏱ 6'**

## S6 · Saliva — the cow's own buffer factory
**SLIDE:**
- A dairy cow produces **100–200 L of saliva per day** *(published range)*
- Saliva contains **bicarbonate and phosphate** — the rumen's main buffer
- Saliva production is driven by **chewing and rumination**, which is driven by **effective fibre (peNDF)**
- Normal **rumination: 7–9 hours/day**; **~450–500 minutes/day** in a healthy lactating cow *(published range)*
- **Falling rumination time is one of the earliest signs of almost any illness** — this is what a sensor measures

**SAY:**
"The cow has her own antacid factory, and it is her salivary glands. She produces a hundred to two hundred litres of saliva a day, and that saliva is loaded with bicarbonate and phosphate.

But — and this is the practical point — saliva only flows when she chews. And she only chews when the ration contains enough **physically effective fibre**: fibre with enough particle length to stimulate rumination.

So follow the chain: long fibre → chewing and rumination → saliva → bicarbonate → stable rumen pH → healthy fibre digesters → acetate → milk fat.

Break the chain at any point and the whole thing collapses. A finely chopped, high-grain ration does not stimulate chewing, so there is no saliva, so there is no buffer, so pH falls.

And note the last line. A healthy lactating cow ruminates about four hundred and fifty to five hundred minutes a day. When she gets sick — any illness — rumination falls before she looks sick to you. That is exactly what a bolus sensor detects, and it is why rumination is the single most useful number in modern herd health."

**BOARD:** Fibre → chewing → saliva → bicarbonate → pH → fibre bugs → acetate → milk fat.

**⏱ 6'**

## S7 · Rumen pH — the number that governs everything
**SLIDE:**
| pH | State |
|---|---|
| **6.2–6.8** | Normal; fibre digestion optimal |
| 5.8–6.2 | Marginal; fibre digestion falling |
| **< 5.6–5.8 for > 3 h/day** | **SARA (subacute ruminal acidosis)** |
| < 5.0–5.2 | **Acute lactic acidosis** — emergency |
- pH varies through the day; **lowest 2–6 hours after a large concentrate meal**
- Diagnosis of SARA: **rumenocentesis** in a sample of cows, or **continuous pH bolus** (modern reference standard)

**SAY:**
"One number governs the whole rumen: pH.

Normal is six point two to six point eight. Below five point six to five point eight for more than about three hours a day, we call it subacute ruminal acidosis — SARA. Below about five, we are in acute lactic acidosis and the cow may die.

Two practical points.

First, pH is not a fixed number. It swings through the day, and the lowest point is two to six hours after a big concentrate meal. So if you measure at the wrong hour, you will miss it entirely.

Second, this is exactly why continuous pH boluses changed the diagnosis of SARA. Before them, you had to do rumenocentesis — a needle through the body wall into the rumen — on a sample of cows, at the right hour, and interpret the herd. Now a bolus reports pH every ten minutes for years. That is the strongest scientific application of sensors in nutrition today."

**⏱ 5'**

## S8 · Protein and nitrogen recycling
**SLIDE:**
- **RDP (rumen degradable protein)** → ammonia → **microbial protein (MCP)** — supplies **50–80% of the cow's metabolisable protein**
- **RUP (rumen undegradable / bypass protein)** → digested in the abomasum and small intestine
- Excess ammonia → absorbed → liver → **urea** → excreted in urine **or recycled to the rumen via saliva and the rumen wall**
- Practical indicator: **milk urea nitrogen (MUN)** — high MUN = protein wasted; low MUN = protein or energy short
**SAY:**
"Briefly on protein, because it explains a feeding mistake I see constantly.

Most of the protein that reaches the cow's intestine is not the protein you fed her. It is **microbial protein** — the bodies of the microbes themselves, washed out of the rumen. Fifty to eighty percent.

To grow those microbes you need two things at the same time: degradable protein, and fermentable energy. If you feed a lot of urea or high-protein forage but no energy, the microbes cannot use the ammonia. It is absorbed, the liver converts it to urea, and the cow spends energy excreting it. You paid for protein and bought a metabolic cost.

And the reverse is also true: cheap energy without protein starves the microbes.

**Feed the microbes, not the cow.** That sentence is the whole of ruminant nutrition."

**⏱ 4'**

## S9 · Gas, eructation and rumen adaptation
**SLIDE:**
- Fermentation produces **CO₂ and methane** — removed by **eructation** driven by secondary reticular contractions
- Blocked eructation → **bloat (tympany)**:
  - **Frothy bloat** — stable foam traps gas (legume/lush pasture, finely ground grain)
  - **Free-gas bloat** — physical or neurological obstruction (oesophageal blockage, **vagal indigestion**)
- **Rumen papillae adapt to diet over 3–6 weeks** — this is why abrupt ration change causes acidosis
**SAY:**
"Two points that have direct clinical consequences.

Fermentation produces a lot of gas, and the cow gets rid of it by belching. Anything that stops belching causes bloat. There are two kinds, and you must distinguish them because the treatment differs: frothy bloat, where the gas is trapped in stable foam and you need an anti-foaming agent; and free-gas bloat, where the gas is free but cannot get out — a blocked oesophagus or damaged vagus nerve — and you need to release it with a stomach tube or trocar.

Second point, and it explains most transition disease. **The rumen papillae — the finger-like projections that absorb VFAs — take three to six weeks to grow when you increase the grain in the ration.**

Now think about what we do to a dairy cow. She is dry, eating forage. She calves. And on the same day we start pushing concentrate to support fifteen or twenty litres of milk. Her papillae are not ready. She produces more acid than she can absorb.

That is why the transition period is the most dangerous three weeks in a dairy cow's life — and it is why the next four diseases all cluster there."

**⏱ 5'**

## S10 · What you can assess in 60 seconds, with no equipment
**SLIDE:**
| Observation | Normal | Abnormal suggests |
|---|---|---|
| **Rumen fill** (left paralumbar fossa) | Filled, slightly hollow triangle | Empty = not eating |
| **Rumen contractions** (hand on left flank, 2 min) | **1–3 per minute** | Reduced = illness, acidosis, hypocalcaemia |
| **Cud chewing** (count cows lying and chewing) | **>60–70% of resting cows chewing** | Low = insufficient effective fibre / illness |
| **Manure consistency** | Firm porridge, holds shape | Loose, bubbly, mucin casts, undigested grain = **SARA** |
| **Manure screening (sieve)** | Few long particles, little grain | Long fibre + whole grain = poor digestion |
| Nose/muzzle | Wet, glistening | Dry = fever, dehydration, illness |
**SAY:**
"Before we go to diseases, this is your field toolkit — and it costs nothing.

Put your fist in the left paralumbar fossa and count contractions for two minutes. One to three per minute is normal.

Stand at the back of the resting group and count how many are chewing their cud. If fewer than sixty percent of lying cows are chewing, that herd does not have enough effective fibre — or it is sick.

Look at the manure. Firm porridge that holds its shape is normal. Loose manure with gas bubbles, with shiny mucin casts, or with whole grains in it, tells you the fermentation is happening in the wrong place — that is SARA until proven otherwise.

You can do all of that in one minute per pen, on every farm you visit, before you even open your AI kit. And it will make you the most useful person on that farm."

**⏱ 5'**

# PART 3-B · ACIDOSIS, SARA, INDIGESTION, BLOAT (25 min, S11–S18)

## S11 · Acute lactic acidosis — mechanism
**SLIDE:**
```
Sudden large intake of readily fermentable carbohydrate (grain, cassava, molasses, bread)
        ↓
Streptococcus bovis proliferates explosively → lactic acid
        ↓  pH falls below ~5.5
S. bovis is inhibited → LACTOBACILLUS takes over → produces D-lactate and L-lactate
        ↓  pH falls below ~5.0
D-lactate is poorly metabolised by the cow → SYSTEMIC METABOLIC ACIDOSIS
        ↓
Rumen stasis + high osmolarity → water pulled INTO the rumen → DEHYDRATION, shock
        ↓
Chemical rumenitis → bacteria/fungi invade the rumen wall → LIVER ABSCESS, laminitis
```
**SAY:**
"Acute acidosis is a chemical accident and I want you to see it as a chain reaction.

A cow gets into the feed store, or eats a wheelbarrow of cassava, or somebody doubles the concentrate. Now there is a huge amount of fermentable carbohydrate in the rumen.

A bacterium called Streptococcus bovis loves this and multiplies explosively, producing lactic acid. As pH falls below about five point five, S. bovis is inhibited — but Lactobacillus, which tolerates acid better, takes over and produces even more lactate, including **D-lactate**.

D-lactate is the dangerous one, because the cow's enzymes handle it poorly. It accumulates in blood and produces systemic metabolic acidosis.

Meanwhile the rumen contents are now extremely concentrated, so water is pulled out of the bloodstream into the rumen. The cow becomes dehydrated even though her rumen is full of fluid. She goes into shock.

And the acid burns the rumen wall — chemical rumenitis — which lets bacteria and fungi cross into the bloodstream. That is where liver abscesses and laminitis come from, days or weeks later."

**⏱ 6'**

## S12 · Acute acidosis — signs, treatment, prevention
**SLIDE:**
| | |
|---|---|
| **Signs** | 12–48 h after the meal: off feed, **rumen stasis**, distended doughy rumen, **profuse watery grey diarrhoea**, dehydration, staggering, tachycardia, sunken eyes, **recumbency**, sometimes blindness (polioencephalomalacia from thiamine deficiency) |
| **Rumen fluid** | pH < 5.0–5.2, milky grey, sour smell, **no protozoa alive** (a rapid field indicator under a microscope) |
| **Treatment (veterinary)** | Remove feed source; **oral alkalinising agent (magnesium hydroxide/oxide)**; **IV fluids with bicarbonate** for systemic acidosis; **thiamine**; NSAID; **rumen lavage** or **rumenotomy** in severe cases; **transfaunation** (rumen fluid from a healthy cow) to restore flora |
| **Prevention** | Lock the feed store. Gradual ration transition. Never a sudden increase in concentrate. Adequate effective fibre. |
**SAY:**
"The signs come twelve to forty-eight hours after the meal, not immediately. Rumen stasis, a doughy distended rumen, and profuse grey watery diarrhoea with a sour smell. The cow is dehydrated, staggering, and in severe cases down.

Note one field diagnostic that is genuinely useful and cheap: take rumen fluid, put a drop under a microscope, and look for protozoa. In a healthy rumen they are large and actively moving. In acute acidosis they are all dead. That one observation, plus a pH strip, gives you a diagnosis in two minutes.

Treatment is veterinary and aggressive — alkalinising agents, intravenous fluids, thiamine, and in severe cases lavage or surgery. Note thiamine: acidosis destroys thiamine-producing bacteria and produces thiaminases, and thiamine deficiency causes brain damage. That is why an acidotic cow can go blind.

But the real lesson is prevention, and it is embarrassingly simple: **lock the feed store, and never change the ration suddenly.**"

**⏱ 5'**

## S13 · ★ SARA — subacute ruminal acidosis
**SLIDE:**
**Definition:** rumen pH **below ~5.6–5.8 for more than ~3 hours per day** — without acute illness
**Key difference from acute:** accumulation of **VFAs**, not primarily lactate — so no systemic acidosis, no dramatic collapse
**Prevalence:** reported in **~10–30% of cows in early lactation** in intensive herds *(published range; herd-dependent)*
**Why it matters:** it is **invisible, chronic and expensive** — the cow never looks acutely sick

**SAY:**
"Now the disease that costs the dairy industry more than any other single condition, and that almost nobody sees: subacute ruminal acidosis.

The definition is a rumen pH below about five point six for more than three hours a day. Note what is different from acute acidosis: here it is mainly VFAs accumulating, not lactate. So there is no systemic acidosis and no collapse. The cow does not fall down. She just quietly performs badly.

Published prevalence in intensive herds is ten to thirty percent of cows in early lactation. In herds that feed a lot of concentrate and not enough effective fibre, it is higher.

Please understand why I am giving this a whole section in a reproduction course. SARA reduces intake, reduces energy, causes laminitis, causes liver abscesses, and worsens negative energy balance — and negative energy balance is the direct cause of the anovulation we spent Module Two trying to treat with hormones."

**⏱ 5'**

## S14 · SARA — how to recognise it in a herd
**SLIDE:**
| Sign | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Milk fat depression** | **Fat:protein ratio < 1.0–1.05** in early lactation cows; herd bulk fat falling |
| Variable / cyclical intake | Cows go on and off feed |
| **Manure** | Loose, bubbly/foamy, **mucin casts (shiny films)**, **undigested grain**, variable between cows in the same pen |
| Low rumination | < ~450 min/day; **sensor-detectable** |
| **Laminitis** | Weeks to months later — sole haemorrhage, white line disease, sole ulcer |
| Liver abscess | Found at slaughter; poor growth, sudden drops in production |
| Poor body condition despite good ration | Energy is being lost |
| **Diagnosis** | **Rumenocentesis** in 12 cows 2–6 h after feeding: **≥3 with pH < 5.5 = herd SARA**; or **continuous pH bolus** |
**SAY:**
"SARA is a herd diagnosis, not an individual one. Here is how you find it.

Start with milk. In early-lactation cows, if the fat-to-protein ratio drops below about one point zero, suspect SARA. Remember slide five — fibre makes acetate, acetate makes butterfat. When the fibre digesters die, butterfat falls first.

Then look at manure, and look at **variation** between cows in the same pen eating the same ration. That variation is the signature of sorting: the aggressive cows eat the concentrate and the timid cows eat the leftover fibre.

Look for mucin casts — shiny films in the manure. That is intestinal mucus shed because fermentation moved to the hindgut.

Then confirm. The classical method is rumenocentesis in a sample of twelve cows, two to six hours after feeding: if three or more have a pH below five point five, the herd has SARA. The modern method is a continuous pH bolus, which removes all the guessing about timing.

And laminitis. If a farm has recurrent hoof problems and nobody can explain them, look at the ration. Lameness of nutritional origin destroys estrus expression — the lame cow will not stand to be mounted. So SARA reaches your reproductive results through at least three separate paths."

**⏱ 6'**

## S15 · SARA — prevention (this is the treatment)
**SLIDE:**
| Measure | Target |
|---|---|
| **Physically effective NDF (peNDF)** | **≥ 20–22% of ration DM** *(system-dependent)* |
| Forage particle length | Adequate long particles; **avoid over-chopping** |
| **Prevent sorting** | TMR moisture ~45–50%; correct chop length; push up feed frequently |
| Feeding frequency | More frequent, smaller meals; **never slug-feed concentrate** |
| Transition | Increase concentrate **gradually over 3–4 weeks** (papillae adaptation) |
| Buffers | **Sodium bicarbonate ~0.75–1.0% of ration DM** where used |
| Live yeast (*S. cerevisiae*) | Stabilises pH by competing for lactate/sugars *(where registered)* |
| Water | Unlimited clean water — dehydration concentrates rumen acid |
| Bunk space / overcrowding | Adequate space so timid cows eat a balanced meal |
**SAY:**
"There is no drug for SARA. The treatment is the ration and the feeding management.

The core number is physically effective fibre — at least twenty to twenty-two percent of dry matter, with enough particle length to make her chew.

Then, prevent sorting. In a smallholder system where concentrate is fed separately from forage, sorting is automatic — the cow eats the concentrate first, in one meal. That single meal is the acid load. If you can feed the concentrate in two or three smaller meals, or mix it with the forage, you have solved most of the problem for free.

Buffers and live yeast help, where they are available and registered. But they are supplements to good feeding, not substitutes for it.

And water. A dehydrated cow has a more concentrated, more acidic rumen. In tropical conditions, water availability is a rumen health measure, not just a comfort measure."

**⏱ 5'**

## S16 · Simple indigestion
**SLIDE:**
- **Cause:** dietary upset — mouldy or spoiled feed, sudden feed change, excessive poor-quality roughage, indigestible material
- **Signs:** partial off-feed, **reduced rumen motility**, mild drop in milk, firm rumen contents, **no fever, no systemic illness**
- **Treatment (veterinary):** remove the offending feed, good hay, oral rumen stimulants/laxatives, **transfaunation**, ensure water
- **Rule:** if the cow has **fever, is truly systemically ill, or does not improve in 24–48 h — it is not simple indigestion.** Look for metritis, mastitis, ketosis, DA, traumatic reticuloperitonitis
**SAY:**
"Simple indigestion is the mildest of the group: the cow ate something she should not have, the rumen slows, she eats less for a day or two, milk drops a little, and with correction she recovers.

The reason I teach it is the rule on the last line, and it is a rule that will make you look very professional.

**If a cow you called 'indigestion' has a fever, or is genuinely sick, or has not improved in twenty-four to forty-eight hours, it was never simple indigestion.** It is metritis, or mastitis, or ketosis, or a displaced abomasum, or hardware disease. 'Indigestion' is the label people put on a cow nobody examined properly.

Give it forty-eight hours, and then insist on a proper examination."

**⏱ 4'**

## S17 · Vagal indigestion (Hoflund syndrome)
**SLIDE:**
Failure of reticuloruminal motility or outflow, usually from **damage to the vagus nerve** or local inflammation/adhesion
| Type | Lesion | Key sign |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Failure of eructation | **Chronic free-gas bloat** |
| **II** | Omasal transport failure (often after **traumatic reticuloperitonitis** — hardware) | Rumen distension, scant manure |
| **III** | **Abomasal impaction** | Large firm abomasum, severe distension |
| **IV** | Partial forestomach obstruction in **late pregnancy** | Progressive distension, improves after calving |
- Classic appearance: **"papple" shape — 10-to-4 abdomen** (left dorsal + right ventral distension)
- **Prognosis is generally poor**; refer early
**SAY:**
"Vagal indigestion is chronic and it is what people mean when they say 'this cow has been bloated for three weeks.'

The vagus nerve controls forestomach motility. Damage it — most commonly by a wire penetrating the reticulum and causing local inflammation and adhesions — and the forestomach cannot move contents forward.

The classic look, and I want you to remember the picture, is the **'papple' abdomen** — shaped like an apple on one side and a pear on the other. From behind, the left upper quadrant and the right lower quadrant bulge. People describe it as a ten-to-four shape, like clock hands.

Prognosis is generally poor, so the value of recognising it is to refer early rather than treat it for weeks as 'indigestion'."

**⏱ 4'**

## S18 · Bloat
**SLIDE:**
| | **Frothy (primary)** | **Free-gas (secondary)** |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Legume/lush pasture, finely ground grain → **stable foam** | Obstruction: oesophageal foreign body, **vagal indigestion**, tetanus, hypocalcaemia, milk fever |
| Stomach tube | Gas does **not** escape (foam) | **Gas escapes** — diagnostic and therapeutic |
| Treatment | **Anti-foaming agent** (poloxalene, vegetable/mineral oil) via tube; walk the cow | Relieve obstruction; pass tube; **trocar** if emergency |
| Emergency | Severe distension + respiratory distress → **trocar/rumenotomy** to save life |
- **Prevention:** avoid turning hungry cows onto lush legume pasture; feed hay before turnout; avoid finely ground grain; anti-bloat agents where indicated
**SAY:**
"Bloat kills quickly, so the field decision matters.

Pass a stomach tube. That single act both diagnoses and treats. If gas rushes out, it was free-gas bloat and you have just saved the cow — now find the obstruction. If nothing comes out but the rumen is enormously distended, it is frothy bloat, and you need an anti-foaming agent, not a tube.

And in a true emergency — a cow going down with severe respiratory distress — a trocar into the left paralumbar fossa is life-saving and is preferable to a dead cow. That is a veterinary decision, but you should know it exists.

Prevention is simple and it is the same everywhere: never turn a hungry cow onto lush legume pasture. Feed hay first."

**⏱ 4'**

# PART 3-C · DISPLACED ABOMASUM (20 min, S19–S22)

## S19 · The abomasum and why it moves
**SLIDE:**
- The abomasum is the **true stomach**, normally on the **right ventral** floor of the abdomen
- It is **only loosely attached** — mobility is normal
- Two conditions must combine for displacement:
  1. **Abomasal hypomotility (atony)** → gas accumulates and the organ floats
  2. **Space in the abdomen** — after calving, the uterus shrinks and rumen fill drops
**SAY:**
"To understand displaced abomasum you only need two facts.

Fact one: the abomasum is not firmly fixed. It hangs relatively loosely, and it normally sits low on the right side.

Fact two: for it to displace, two things must happen at the same time. It must stop contracting properly, so that gas accumulates and it becomes buoyant. And there must be space for it to move into.

Now think about the fresh cow. She has just calved, so the uterus that occupied half her abdomen has suddenly shrunk. Her intake dropped before calving, so her rumen is less full. There is a large empty space. And if she is hypocalcaemic or ketotic, her abomasum is atonic and full of gas.

That is why eighty to ninety percent of displacements happen in the first month after calving. It is not bad luck. It is predictable."

**⏱ 5'**

## S20 · LDA — left displaced abomasum
**SLIDE:**
| | |
|---|---|
| **Mechanism** | Atonic gas-filled abomasum migrates from right ventral, under the rumen, to the **left flank between rumen and body wall** |
| **Epidemiology** | **~1–5% of cows** in many herds, higher in problem herds; **80–90% within 30 days of calving** *(published ranges)* |
| **Risk factors** | **Hypocalcaemia**, **ketosis / high NEFA**, **metritis**, **retained placenta**, twins, dystocia, **low DMI before calving**, high grain / low effective fibre, over-conditioned dry cows (BCS >3.75), abrupt ration change, overcrowding |
| **Signs** | Sudden **milk drop**, reduced/selective appetite (refuses grain), **ketosis**, scant pasty manure, "slab-sided" left flank, mild dehydration |
| **Diagnosis** | **Simultaneous auscultation and percussion** — high-pitched **"PING"** over ribs **9–12 on the LEFT**; ultrasound confirms; blood BHB usually elevated |
| **Treatment (veterinary)** | **Surgery**: right flank omentopexy/pyloropexy, left flank abomasopexy (Utrecht), or right paramedian abomasopexy; **or roll-and-toggle**. **Always treat the concurrent ketosis, hypocalcaemia and metritis** |
**SAY:**
"Left displacement is by far the more common one.

Look at the risk factor list and notice something important: almost every item on it is another disease we are discussing today. Hypocalcaemia. Ketosis. Metritis. Retained placenta. Low intake. This is not a separate disease. **It is the visible failure of the transition period.**

Recognition. The cow calved two or three weeks ago. Her milk suddenly drops. She refuses grain but will eat some hay — that selective appetite is characteristic. She has ketosis that does not respond well to treatment. Her left flank looks flat instead of full.

Then the diagnostic act, and every technician should be able to do it: put the stethoscope over the left flank, ribs nine to twelve, and flick the body wall with your finger. A high-pitched metallic ping — like flicking a balloon — is a displaced abomasum until proven otherwise.

Treatment is surgical, and it works well when done early. But the point I want to leave you with is the last line of the table: **you must also treat the ketosis, the calcium and the uterus.** Surgeons who only fix the abomasum get cows that relapse."

**BOARD:** Draw the left flank, ribs 9–12, mark the ping location.

**⏱ 7'**

## S21 · RDA and abomasal volvulus
**SLIDE:**
| | **RDA (right dilatation)** | **Abomasal volvulus (AV)** |
|---|---|---|
| Lesion | Abomasum dilates on the right, no torsion | **Rotates on its mesenteric axis → vascular compromise** |
| Onset | Gradual | **Acute, dramatic** |
| Signs | Off feed, milk drop, right-side ping (ribs 10–13) | **Severe colic, tachycardia >100 bpm, dehydration, shock**, distended right abdomen, scant dark manure |
| Blood | Mild changes | **Hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic METABOLIC ALKALOSIS** (HCl sequestered in the abomasum) + **paradoxical aciduria** |
| Prognosis | Good if early | **Guarded to poor**; heart rate and duration are the best predictors |
| Treatment | Surgery | **EMERGENCY surgery + aggressive IV isotonic saline with KCl** |
> **Right-side ping — differential list:** RDA/AV · caecal dilatation or volvulus · spiral colon gas · pneumoperitoneum · rectal/uterine gas
**SAY:**
"Right displacement is less common but far more dangerous, because it can progress to volvulus — the abomasum twists on its own axis and cuts off its blood supply.

Note the metabolic picture, because it is the opposite of what people expect. The abomasum makes hydrochloric acid. When it is twisted, that acid is sequestered and cannot pass on to the intestine to be reabsorbed. So the cow loses chloride and hydrogen ions from her circulation and becomes **alkalotic**, not acidotic. She also loses potassium. That is why the fluid therapy is isotonic saline with added potassium chloride — and why giving bicarbonate to such a cow would be a serious error.

Two field markers of severity, and you can assess both: **heart rate above one hundred, and how long she has been sick.** Both predict a poor outcome. A cow with a right-side ping and a heart rate of one hundred and twenty needs a veterinarian now, not tomorrow.

And remember that not every right-side ping is an abomasum. Caecal dilatation gives a ping too, further back. Say 'right-side ping' and list the differentials — do not say 'RDA' until someone has examined her."

**⏱ 6'**

## S22 · Preventing displacement — the transition programme
**SLIDE:**
| Measure | Target |
|---|---|
| **BCS at calving** | **3.0–3.5**, never above 3.75; avoid fat dry cows |
| Dry period ration | **Controlled energy**, bulky, straw-based; keep the rumen full |
| **Effective fibre in fresh cows** | Maintain rumen fill; increase concentrate gradually |
| **Prevent hypocalcaemia** | DCAD programme (S26–S28), oral Ca for high-risk cows |
| **Prevent ketosis** | Transition nutrition, monitor BHB |
| **Control metritis/RP** | Clean calving pen, hygiene at assistance |
| **DMI before calving** | Avoid the pre-calving intake drop: adequate bunk space (**~76 cm/cow**), **stocking <80–85%**, minimal pen moves, no heat stress |
> **You do not prevent DA by watching for pings. You prevent it by managing the three weeks before calving.**
**SAY:**
"And here is the strategic point of this whole section.

Nobody prevents displaced abomasum by getting better at finding pings. You prevent it by managing dry matter intake in the three weeks before calving and the three weeks after.

The single most predictive thing you can measure on a farm is whether the fresh cow is eating. If intake stays up, calcium stays up, ketosis is mild, the rumen stays full, the abomasum stays where it belongs, and the uterus cleans itself.

Look at that last row. Bunk space. Stocking density. Pen moves. Heat stress. Those are not veterinary interventions. They are management decisions, and they are free.

So when a farmer asks you why he keeps getting displaced abomasums, do not talk about surgery. **Ask to see where his dry cows eat.**"

**⏱ 5'**

# PART 3-D · MILK FEVER AND KETOSIS (25 min, S23–S29)

## S23 · Milk fever — the calcium accident
**SLIDE:**
```
At calving: colostrum contains ~2.3 g Ca/L → 10 L = ~23 g Ca leaves the blood in hours
Total circulating Ca pool ≈ 3 g
        ↓ demand is ~8–10× the pool
Homeostasis must respond: PTH → 1,25(OH)₂ vitamin D₃
        → intestinal Ca absorption ↑ (takes 24–48 h) + bone resorption ↑
        ↓ IF THE RESPONSE FAILS
HYPOCALCAEMIA → smooth and skeletal muscle weakness
```
**SAY:**
"Milk fever is a mathematical accident, and once you see the numbers you will never forget it.

Colostrum contains a lot of calcium — about two point three grams per litre. A cow producing ten litres of colostrum loses about twenty-three grams of calcium into the udder within hours.

Her entire circulating calcium pool is about three grams.

So the demand is eight to ten times everything she has in circulation, and it arrives in a few hours. She must mobilise calcium from bone and absorb more from the intestine, and both processes are driven by parathyroid hormone and activated vitamin D.

The problem is that this system takes twenty-four to forty-eight hours to reach full capacity. If it was not already primed before calving, she falls behind. And calcium is what makes muscle contract — all muscle. So she becomes weak."

**⏱ 5'**

## S24 · ★ Why the prepartum diet decides it — DCAD
**SLIDE:**
- A **metabolic alkalosis** before calving (from **high potassium and high cation forages**) changes the conformation of the **PTH receptor** → **tissue insensitivity to PTH** → the cow cannot mobilise calcium even though PTH is high
- **DCAD** = (Na⁺ + K⁺) − (Cl⁻ + S²⁻), mEq/kg DM
- **Prepartum target: negative DCAD, about −100 to −150 mEq/kg DM** *(system-dependent)*
- **Verify by urine pH** — the practical field check: **Holstein 6.0–6.5**, **Jersey 5.5–6.0** *(published targets)*
- **Magnesium is essential** — Mg is required for PTH secretion and action; target **~0.4% of DM**
- Avoid **high-potassium forages** for dry cows
**SAY:**
"Now the part that separates modern prevention from the old approach, and I want you to understand it because it is genuinely elegant.

For decades people thought milk fever was about how much calcium you fed before calving. It is not. It is about **acid-base balance**.

If the dry cow's diet is high in potassium and other cations — which tropical forages very often are, especially grass fertilised with manure — she goes into a mild metabolic alkalosis. In alkalosis, the parathyroid hormone **receptor** changes shape and the tissues stop responding to PTH. Her blood is full of PTH and her bones ignore it.

So we feed anionic salts before calving to shift her slightly acidic. That restores receptor sensitivity, and she can mobilise her own calcium.

And here is the beautiful part for field work: you can verify it with a urine test strip. Collect urine from a few dry cows and measure pH. In Holsteins, six point zero to six point five means the programme is working. Above seven means she is still alkaline and your anionic salts are not doing their job. Below five point five means you have overshot and she will stop eating.

A urine pH strip costs almost nothing and it monitors the most expensive metabolic disease in dairy. Teach every farm you visit to do it.

And do not forget magnesium. PTH cannot be secreted or act properly without adequate magnesium. A magnesium-deficient cow gets milk fever no matter how good your DCAD is."

**BOARD:** Write "High K → alkalosis → PTH receptor insensitive → milk fever." Then "Anionic salts → urine pH 6.0–6.5 → receptor works."

**⏱ 7'**

## S25 · Milk fever — stages, treatment, and the subclinical iceberg
**SLIDE:**
| Stage | Signs |
|---|---|
| **I** | Still standing; excitement, hypersensitivity, muscle tremor, ataxia, mild ruminal stasis — **often missed** |
| **II** | **Sternal recumbency**, **S-curved neck / head turned to flank**, cold extremities, **dry muzzle**, dilated pupils, tachycardia with weak pulse, rumen stasis, no defecation |
| **III** | Lateral recumbency, coma, severe bloat, death |
**Epidemiology:** clinical **~3–8%** of calvings; **subclinical hypocalcaemia 25–50% of multiparous cows** *(published ranges)*. Risk rises with **parity ≥3** and in **Jersey** cattle.
**Treatment (veterinary):** **IV calcium borogluconate ~500 mL slowly over 8–10 min with cardiac auscultation** — too fast causes fatal arrhythmia; follow with **oral calcium boluses**; subcutaneous calcium as an adjunct.
**Consequences of subclinical hypocalcaemia:** ↑ retained placenta, ↑ metritis, ↑ **displaced abomasum**, ↑ ketosis, ↓ intake, ↓ immune function, **↓ fertility**
**SAY:**
"Three stages. Stage one is standing and excitable, and it is usually missed. Stage two is the classic picture: sitting up, head turned back to the flank in an S-curve, cold ears, dry muzzle. Stage three is flat out and dying.

Treatment is intravenous calcium — and I want to make one safety point strongly. Calcium given rapidly intravenously **stops the heart**. It must go in slowly, over eight to ten minutes, ideally while listening to the heart. A cow that dies on the needle during a milk fever treatment died of the treatment, not the disease. This is a veterinary procedure.

But the real message of this slide is the second line of the epidemiology. Clinical milk fever is three to eight percent. **Subclinical hypocalcaemia is twenty-five to fifty percent of older cows.**

Those cows never go down. They just have weak uterine contractions, so they retain the placenta and get metritis. They have a weak teat sphincter, so they get mastitis. They have an atonic abomasum, so it displaces. They eat less, so they get ketosis. And they do not conceive.

So when I say that calcium is a reproductive hormone in the dairy cow, I mean it literally."

**⏱ 6'**

## S26 · ★ Ketosis — the mechanism
**SLIDE:**
```
Early lactation: energy output (milk) > energy input (intake) = NEGATIVE ENERGY BALANCE
        ↓
Body fat mobilised → NEFA (non-esterified fatty acids) → LIVER
        ↓
Liver must β-oxidise NEFA. Complete oxidation needs OXALOACETATE.
BUT oxaloacetate is being consumed for GLUCONEOGENESIS (to make lactose for milk)
        ↓
Incomplete β-oxidation → KETONE BODIES: BHB, acetoacetate, acetone
        ↓ and simultaneously
NEFA re-esterified to triglyceride faster than VLDL can export it → FATTY LIVER
```
**SAY:**
"Ketosis is the most important metabolic disease in dairy, and its mechanism is a competition inside the liver. Follow me carefully.

In early lactation the cow produces more energy in milk than she can eat. That is negative energy balance, and to some degree it is normal.

To cover the gap she mobilises body fat, which arrives at the liver as non-esterified fatty acids — NEFA.

The liver burns fat by beta-oxidation, and to burn it completely it needs **oxaloacetate**. Here is the conflict: oxaloacetate is also the raw material for making glucose — and the cow desperately needs glucose to make lactose for milk.

So the liver has to choose. It sends oxaloacetate to glucose production, and now there is not enough left to complete fat oxidation. Fat oxidation stops halfway, and the half-burned products are the **ketone bodies** — beta-hydroxybutyrate, acetoacetate and acetone.

And at the same time, any NEFA the liver cannot burn gets repackaged as triglyceride. The cow's liver exports triglyceride very poorly — cattle are bad at making VLDL. So the fat stays in the liver. **That is fatty liver, and fatty liver and ketosis are the same disease seen from two angles.**

Now — remember Module Two. Which cow was anovular? The cow in negative energy balance. This slide is the mechanism of the infertility we tried to treat with hormones this morning."

**BOARD:** Draw the liver with two arrows leaving oxaloacetate: one to glucose, one to complete fat oxidation. Show the competition.

**⏱ 7'**

## S27 · Ketosis — Type I and Type II
**SLIDE:**
| | **Type I** | **Type II** |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | **3–6 weeks** post-calving | **First 1–2 weeks** post-calving |
| Underlying problem | **Glucose deficiency** — intake genuinely insufficient at peak milk | **Insulin resistance + fatty liver** — the over-conditioned cow |
| Insulin / glucose | Low insulin, low glucose | Higher insulin, **insulin resistance**, fatty liver |
| Cow type | Thin, high-producing, under-fed | **Fat at calving (BCS >3.75)** |
| Response to propylene glycol | Good | Poorer; harder to treat |
| Prevention | Maximise intake at peak | **Do not let dry cows get fat** |
**SAY:**
"There are two ketoses and they need different prevention.

Type one is the classic: three to six weeks after calving, at peak milk, the cow simply cannot eat enough. She is thin and working hard. She responds well to glucose precursors.

Type two happens earlier, in the first week or two, and it is the disease of the **fat cow**. She arrived at calving over-conditioned. Fat cows are insulin resistant, they eat less around calving, and they mobilise fat massively. They get fatty liver early, and they respond poorly to treatment.

The practical conclusion for you is blunt: **a fat dry cow is a sick fresh cow.** When you walk onto a farm and see dry cows in body condition four, you can predict that farm's ketosis, milk fever, displaced abomasum and metritis rates before you look at a single record."

**⏱ 5'**

## S28 · Ketosis — diagnosis and epidemiology
**SLIDE:**
| Test | Threshold |
|---|---|
| **Blood BHB (handheld cow-side meter)** — the reference standard | **≥ 1.2 mmol/L = subclinical ketosis (hyperketonaemia)**; **≥ 3.0 mmol/L = clinical** *(widely used thresholds)* |
| Milk BHB strips | Herd screening |
| Urine acetoacetate strips | Cheap; **less specific** (concentration varies with urine dilution) |
| **Milk fat:protein ratio** | **> 1.4** in early lactation suggests excessive fat mobilisation |
**Epidemiology:** subclinical ketosis affects **~20–40% of cows** in the first two weeks *(published range)*; clinical ~2–15%.
**Best testing window: 3–14 days in milk.**
**Signs (clinical):** selective appetite (**refuses concentrate first**), milk drop, rapid BCS loss, dull, firm scant manure, acetone smell on breath; **nervous form** (rare): licking, chewing, staggering, aggression.
**SAY:**
"Diagnosis is now easy and cheap, and this is one of the most useful things you can bring to a farm.

A handheld blood meter — the same kind used for human diabetics, with bovine BHB strips — gives you a number in ten seconds from a drop of blood from the tail vein. One point two millimoles per litre or above is subclinical ketosis. Three or above is clinical.

Test between three and fourteen days in milk. That is the window.

And notice the prevalence: twenty to forty percent of cows in the first two weeks. That means on a well-managed farm you will still find them; on a poorly managed farm, half the fresh pen.

One clinical detail worth remembering: the ketotic cow **refuses concentrate first** and will still eat forage. That is the opposite of most sick cows and it is a useful field observation. And the smell — acetone on the breath, like nail polish remover. Some people cannot smell it at all; it is genetic. If you can smell it, it is a free diagnostic test."

**⏱ 5'**

## S29 · Ketosis — treatment and prevention
**SLIDE:**
| | |
|---|---|
| **Treatment (veterinary)** | **Propylene glycol 300 mL orally once daily for 3–5 days** — the evidence-based standard (a glucose precursor). **IV 50% dextrose 500 mL** gives a rapid but short-lived rise — use with propylene glycol, not alone. Corticosteroid in selected cases (veterinary judgement). B vitamins/cobalt as supportive. **Always treat the underlying cause — DA, metritis, mastitis, lameness.** |
| **Prevention** | BCS at dry-off **3.0–3.5**, never >3.75 · **controlled-energy dry cow ration** (bulky, straw-based) · maximise fresh cow DMI: **bunk space ~76 cm/cow, stocking <80–85%, minimal pen moves, cool, clean water** · adequate effective fibre · **monitor BHB at 3–14 DIM** and act on the herd number · ionophores where registered · rumen-protected choline in selected programmes · **heat abatement** |
**SAY:**
"Treatment: propylene glycol, three hundred millilitres orally, once a day for three to five days. That is the evidence-based standard and it works because it is a glucose precursor that is absorbed and converted to glucose in the liver.

Intravenous dextrose raises blood glucose within minutes, but it is gone within two hours and much of it is lost in urine. So dextrose is for the cow that is down or nervous; propylene glycol is the actual treatment.

And the line I want you to underline: **always treat the underlying cause.** A cow with ketosis secondary to a displaced abomasum or metritis will relapse forever if you only give her propylene glycol. Ketosis is very often a symptom, not the disease.

Prevention is entirely about the dry period and the fresh pen. Do not let dry cows get fat. Feed a controlled-energy, bulky dry-cow ration. And then protect fresh cow intake with space, comfort, water and cooling.

And test. A herd that measures BHB in fresh cows and acts on the number will halve its transition disease. That is a service you can offer on every farm you visit — and it makes you a herd advisor, not just an inseminator."

**⏱ 5'**

# PART 3-E · CALVING AND UTERINE DISEASE (25 min, S30–S45)

## S30 · The three stages of labour
**SLIDE:**
| Stage | Duration | What is happening |
|---|---|---|
| **I — Cervical dilation** | **2–6 h** (longer in heifers) | Restlessness, tail raised, isolation, uterine contractions begin; **cervix must dilate fully** |
| **II — Fetal expulsion** | **Cows 30–60 min; heifers 1–4 h** | Water bag, then feet; abdominal press; calf delivered |
| **III — Placental expulsion** | **Within 6–12 h** | Placentomes detach |
> **Intervention rule:** examine if **stage II exceeds ~60–70 minutes in a cow** or **~2 hours in a heifer**, or **if there is no progress for 20–30 minutes** once the feet are visible.
**SAY:**
"Before dystocia, the normal picture — because you cannot recognise abnormal without it.

Stage one is cervical dilation, two to six hours, and this is the stage people miss. The cow is restless, isolates herself, lifts her tail. Nothing is visible yet, and **the cervix is not yet open.**

That last point matters enormously, because the most damaging mistake in calving management is to intervene during stage one. If you pull on a calf through an undilated cervix, you tear the cervix, and that cow may never conceive again.

Stage two is delivery: thirty to sixty minutes in a cow, up to a few hours in a heifer.

Stage three is the placenta, within six to twelve hours.

And the modern intervention rule is on the last line, and it has changed: **examine early, intervene appropriately.** Waiting six hours 'to let nature take its course' produces dead calves and damaged cows. But examining does not mean pulling."

**⏱ 5'**

## S31 · Dystocia — causes and epidemiology
**SLIDE:**
| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Fetal-maternal disproportion** | Oversized calf; small/immature heifer; **bred too young or too small** |
| **Abnormal presentation / position / posture** | Posterior presentation; head turned back; carpal or shoulder flexion; breech |
| **Maternal** | **Uterine inertia (frequently HYPOCALCAEMIA)**; incomplete cervical dilation; **uterine torsion**; pelvic abnormality |
| **Fetal** | Twins entangled; fetal monsters; hydrocephalus; fetal death and emphysema |
**Epidemiology:** roughly **2–7% in cows, 10–25%+ in heifers**, herd-dependent *(published ranges)*
**Consequences:** stillbirth, calf hypoxia, **retained placenta, metritis**, obturator nerve paralysis, uterine/cervical tears, **delayed conception, culling**
**SAY:**
"Three groups of causes, and you should classify every difficult calving you attend.

Disproportion — the calf is too big or the heifer is too small. This is a **breeding decision made twelve months earlier**, and it is why heifer size at first breeding and calving-ease sire selection are reproductive tools.

Malposture — the head is back, a leg is flexed, the calf is backwards. These are correctable by hand if you have space, lubricant and patience.

Maternal — and here is the link to the last section. **Uterine inertia is very often hypocalcaemia.** A cow with subclinical milk fever cannot contract her uterus. So a proportion of what farms call dystocia is actually a calcium problem, and it is prevented in the dry pen with DCAD, not in the calving pen with a rope."

**⏱ 5'**

## S32 · Dystocia — examination and correction
**SLIDE:**
1. **Restrain safely.** Clean the perineum. **Wash hands and arms, use plenty of clean lubricant.**
2. Determine: **presentation** (anterior/posterior), **position** (dorsosacral is normal), **posture** (head and limbs extended)
3. **Confirm the cervix is fully dilated** before any traction
4. Correct malposture **in the uterus, with the calf repelled forward** — never try to correct in the pelvis
5. **Traction rules:** apply **only during the cow's contractions**; **maximum two people** (or a calving jack used with equivalent restraint); **alternate the legs** to walk the shoulders through the pelvis; **rotate the calf ~45°** for hip lock; **direct traction downward** once the head is out
6. **Stop and call the veterinarian** if: no progress in **10–15 minutes** of correct traction, cervix not dilated, irreducible malposture, **uterine torsion**, fetal oversize, emphysematous fetus → **caesarean or fetotomy**
**SAY:**
"Rules that will save calves and cows.

Lubricant. Use far more than you think you need. Most field damage is friction damage.

Confirm the cervix is open before you pull anything. Put your hand around it. If you cannot get your hand past it, do not pull.

Correct malposture by pushing the calf forward into the uterus first, where there is room. Trying to straighten a leg while the calf is jammed in the pelvis tears the uterus.

Traction: pull only when she pushes, and pull with two people. A calving jack multiplies force enormously and can fracture the calf's ribs, tear the uterus, or cause obturator nerve paralysis so that the cow cannot stand afterwards. If two people and correct technique are not moving the calf in ten to fifteen minutes, the answer is not more force. The answer is a caesarean section.

And say this to farmers: **a live calf and a healthy cow from a caesarean is a better outcome than a dead calf and a paralysed cow from an hour of pulling.**"

**⏱ 6'**

## S33 · Uterine torsion and calf resuscitation
**SLIDE:**
**Uterine torsion**
- Suspect when: stage II is prolonged with **no water bag**, and vaginal examination finds **spiral folds** narrowing the passage
- Correction: **rolling the cow** (with a plank across the abdomen), **detorsion rod**, or **caesarean section**
**Calf resuscitation**
- **Clear the airway** (wipe nose and mouth; suction if available)
- **Do NOT hang the calf upside down for more than a few seconds** — abdominal organs compress the diaphragm and worsen breathing
- Place in **sternal recumbency**; stimulate with vigorous rubbing, cold water on the head/ear, straw in the nostril
- **Colostrum: 3–4 L of good quality within the first 2 hours**, by bottle or oesophageal tube
**SAY:**
"Two things that come up in every calving season.

Uterine torsion: the classic clue is a cow in second-stage labour with nothing appearing and no water bag. When you examine, the vagina spirals and narrows and you cannot reach the calf. Rolling the cow is a genuine field technique, but it must be done correctly and is a veterinary procedure.

And calf resuscitation. Please stop hanging calves upside down over a gate. It was standard for fifty years and it is wrong. A few seconds to drain the mouth is fine; hanging for a minute pushes the abdominal organs against the diaphragm and makes it **harder** for the calf to breathe. Put the calf on its chest — sternal — because that position lets both lungs inflate. Then rub it hard, put cold water in its ear, and put straw up its nose.

Then colostrum. Three to four litres within two hours. Every hour of delay reduces absorption. This calf may be your imported embryo calf — a genetic investment that took a year and a laboratory to create. Do not lose it in the first two hours."

**⏱ 5'**

## S34 · Retained placenta — mechanism and epidemiology
**SLIDE:**
**Definition:** fetal membranes not expelled within **12–24 hours** after calving
**Mechanism:**
1. **Failure of placentome detachment** — normally collagen in the caruncle–cotyledon attachment breaks down before calving
2. **Immune failure** — neutrophil (PMN) chemotaxis and function are suppressed around calving; leukocytes normally help release the membranes
3. **Uterine inertia** — poor contractions (hypocalcaemia)
**Risk factors:** **dystocia · twins · abortion · stillbirth · induced parturition · milk fever/hypocalcaemia · short or long gestation · heat stress · selenium and vitamin E deficiency · vitamin A deficiency · over-conditioned cows**
**Epidemiology:** normal **4–10%**; **>10% = herd problem** *(published ranges)*
**SAY:**
"Retained placenta looks like a mechanical problem and it is not. It is largely an **immune** problem.

Around calving, the cow's neutrophils — her front-line white blood cells — become suppressed. That suppression is worsened by hypocalcaemia, by high NEFA from fat mobilisation, and by deficiency of selenium and vitamin E. Those white cells are part of how the placenta is released, so an immunosuppressed cow retains her membranes.

Look at the risk factor list again and count how many we have already covered today: dystocia, hypocalcaemia, heat stress, over-conditioning. Again — it is one disease with many names.

The herd threshold is important for your consulting work: above ten percent, that farm has a transition management problem, not a run of bad luck."

**⏱ 5'**

## S35 · Retained placenta — what to do and what NOT to do
**SLIDE:**
| **DO NOT** | **DO** |
|---|---|
| ❌ **Do not manually remove the placenta** — it damages caruncles, causes bacteraemia, and **increases metritis** | ✅ Trim hanging membranes to prevent contamination |
| ❌ Do not routinely infuse intrauterine antibiotics or irritants | ✅ **Monitor rectal temperature daily for 10 days** |
| ❌ Do not treat every RP with systemic antibiotics | ✅ **Systemic antibiotics (veterinary) only if she is febrile / systemically ill** |
| ❌ Do not ignore her | ✅ Support calcium and energy; ensure intake and water |
**Prevention:** DCAD/hypocalcaemia control · **selenium and vitamin E supplementation** (essential in Se-deficient regions) · vitamin A/E · avoid dystocia · control heat stress · correct BCS · **clean, dry, individual calving area**
**SAY:**
"This slide is the one I most want you to carry away from this section, because the wrong practice is nearly universal.

**Do not pull the placenta out.** I know it is traditional. I know it looks better afterwards. But manual removal tears the caruncles, opens blood vessels to bacteria, and multiple studies show it **increases** metritis and delays conception. The membranes will come away on their own in a few days.

What you do instead: trim what is hanging so it does not drag through manure, and then **take her temperature every day for ten days**. A retained placenta with a normal temperature and a cow that is eating needs supportive care, not antibiotics. A retained placenta with a fever above thirty-nine point five and a sick cow is metritis, and that needs veterinary treatment today.

That single change in practice — stop pulling, start taking temperatures — will improve the fertility of every farm you advise."

**⏱ 6'**

## S36 · Metritis
**SLIDE:**
| | **Puerperal metritis** | **Clinical metritis** |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | **≤ 21 DIM** | ≤ 21 DIM |
| Uterus | Enlarged | Enlarged |
| Discharge | **Fetid, watery, red-brown ("chocolate")** | Same |
| Systemic signs | **Fever ≥ 39.5 °C, dull, off feed, milk drop** | **Absent** |
| Treatment (veterinary) | **Systemic antibiotic (e.g. ceftiofur per label)** + NSAID + fluids/supportive care | Often monitored; treat per veterinary judgement |
**Pathogens:** *Escherichia coli* (early) → ***Trueperella pyogenes*** + ***Fusobacterium necrophorum*** + *Prevotella* (synergistic; this combination causes the damage)
**Epidemiology:** ~**10–20%** of calvings; higher after RP, dystocia, twins, stillbirth *(published ranges)*
**SAY:**
"Metritis is inflammation of the whole uterine wall in the first three weeks after calving, and the distinction that matters clinically is whether she is systemically sick.

Puerperal metritis — fever, dull, off feed, milk down, with that unmistakable watery red-brown fetid discharge — is a systemic illness and needs systemic antibiotic treatment by a veterinarian. Intrauterine infusions do not reach the deeper uterine layers where the infection is, and irritant infusions make it worse.

The microbiology is worth one sentence because it explains the smell and the damage: E. coli colonises first and prepares the ground, then Trueperella pyogenes and Fusobacterium necrophorum arrive and act synergistically. That combination produces the tissue destruction and the characteristic odour.

Your role: **temperature, discharge, appetite.** Those three observations, recorded daily for the first ten days, catch nearly every case early."

**⏱ 5'**

## S37 · Endometritis — clinical and subclinical
**SLIDE:**
| | **Clinical endometritis** | **Subclinical endometritis** |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | **> 21 DIM** | 21–33 DIM |
| Signs | **Purulent (>50% pus) discharge**, or mucopurulent after 26 DIM. **No systemic illness** | **No visible discharge** |
| Diagnosis | **Vaginoscopy or Metricheck device**; vaginal mucus scoring | **Endometrial cytology (cytobrush)** — elevated **PMN %** (thresholds ~≥5–10% at 21–33 DIM, criteria vary) |
| Prevalence | ~**10–20%** | ~**11–30%** *(published ranges)* |
| Treatment (veterinary) | **Intrauterine cephapirin** (where licensed), or **PGF2α if a CL is present** | Often PGF2α-based; evidence varies |
> **Both reduce conception rate and increase days open — subclinical endometritis is invisible without a test.**
**SAY:**
"After twenty-one days, we stop calling it metritis and call it endometritis — inflammation limited to the lining.

Clinical endometritis you can find with a simple device: a Metricheck, which is a stainless steel rod with a rubber cup that you insert and withdraw to retrieve vaginal mucus, and then you score it. Clear mucus is normal. Flecks of pus, or mucopurulent, or frankly purulent discharge, is disease. That examination takes thirty seconds and costs nothing per cow.

Subclinical endometritis is the invisible one. The cow looks completely normal, the discharge looks normal, and there are excessive white cells in her uterine lining. It affects between eleven and thirty percent of cows and it substantially reduces conception rate.

And here is the point for you: **this is a large part of your repeat breeders.** When you have inseminated a cow three times with good timing and good semen and she will not conceive, the question is not 'should I use a different bull'. The question is 'has anyone examined her uterus'.

One important treatment note: prostaglandin is often used for endometritis, and it only works **if there is a corpus luteum.** Same rule as Module Two. Everything connects."

**⏱ 6'**

## S38 · Pyometra
**SLIDE:**
- **Definition:** accumulation of **pus in the uterine lumen** with a **persistent corpus luteum** and a **closed cervix**
- Typically **after 21 DIM**, often following metritis/endometritis; sometimes after early embryonic death
- **The cow is NOT systemically ill** — she simply does not return to estrus
- **Danger: an enlarged, fluid-filled uterus with a CL can be mistaken for PREGNANCY on palpation** — ultrasound distinguishes them (pus is echogenic/"snowstorm"; no fetus, no placentomes, no heartbeat)
- **Treatment (veterinary): PGF2α** → luteolysis → cervix relaxes → drainage. Repeat in 10–14 days if needed.
- ⚠️ **Confirm she is NOT pregnant before giving PGF2α.**
**SAY:**
"Pyometra is the trap, and I want every one of you to remember the warning line.

The uterus is full of pus. The cervix is closed, so it cannot drain. And because there is pus in the uterus, the uterus never releases prostaglandin, so the corpus luteum persists — the cow stays in a permanent luteal phase and never comes into heat.

Now put yourself in the field. You palpate a cow that has not been seen in heat for two months. You feel an enlarged uterus with fluid, and a corpus luteum on the ovary. **What does that feel like? Pregnancy.**

If you record her as pregnant, she is culled six months later as a phantom pregnancy. If you give prostaglandin without checking, and she actually **was** pregnant, you abort her.

So the rule is absolute: an enlarged fluid-filled uterus with a CL requires **ultrasound** before you do anything. Pus on ultrasound looks like a swirling snowstorm, with no fetus, no placentomes and no heartbeat.

Then treatment is straightforward and satisfying: prostaglandin removes the CL, the cervix opens, and she drains."

## S40 · Heat stress — the multiplier on every disease above
**SLIDE:**
- **THI ≥ 72** production losses begin; **THI ≥ 78** severe reproductive effects *(published thresholds)*
- Effects: **DMI ↓ → NEB ↑ → ketosis ↑** · rumen buffering ↓ (less rumination, more panting → respiratory alkalosis then compensatory bicarbonate loss in urine) → **SARA ↑** · immune suppression → **metritis ↑** · **estrus shorter and weaker** · **oocyte damaged for 40–60 days** · **early embryonic loss ↑** · birth weight ↓
- **Abatement in order of return:** **water → shade → air movement (fans) → sprinkler + fan at the feed line → do all handling before 09:00**
**SAY:**
"And here is why heat stress belongs at the end of this module rather than at the beginning: **it is a multiplier on everything we have discussed.**

Heat reduces intake, which worsens negative energy balance, which causes ketosis. Panting causes respiratory alkalosis, and the cow compensates by dumping bicarbonate in her urine — so she has **less bicarbonate available to buffer her rumen**, which worsens SARA. Notice that: heat stress causes acidosis through a route most people never think about. Heat suppresses immunity, which worsens metritis. And it damages the oocyte for the forty to sixty days before ovulation.

The abatement list is in order of return on investment, and the first item is free: **water**. Enough clean water, in enough places, in the shade. Then shade. Then air movement. Then sprinklers at the feed line. And then the one that costs nothing at all — **do all your work before nine in the morning.**"

## APPENDIX B · Anticipated questions

**Q. "Can I just give calcium to every cow at calving?"**
"Oral calcium boluses at calving are widely used for cows at higher risk — third lactation and above, high producers, and cows with a history. Blanket intravenous calcium is not appropriate and carries cardiac risk. And oral calcium does not replace a prepartum DCAD programme; it supplements it. Discuss the protocol with your veterinarian."

**Q. "How do I know if my ration has enough effective fibre without a laboratory?"**
"Two field tests. First, count cud chewing: more than sixty to seventy percent of resting cows should be chewing. Second, look at manure consistency and screen it — long undigested fibre plus whole grain means the ration is passing through too fast. Both cost nothing."

**Q. "Is propylene glycol better than dextrose?"**
"For treatment of ketosis, yes — propylene glycol given orally once daily for three to five days is the evidence-based standard because it is converted to glucose over hours. Intravenous dextrose gives a rapid but short-lived rise and is best reserved for the down or nervous cow, given together with propylene glycol."

**Q. "Should we pull retained placentas? Everyone here does it."**
"No. I know it is universal, and I know it looks better afterwards. But manual removal damages the caruncles, opens blood vessels to bacteria, and multiple studies show it increases metritis and delays conception. Trim what hangs, take her temperature daily for ten days, and treat only if she is febrile or systemically ill."

**Q. "My farm has many repeat breeders. What is the first thing to check?"**
"In this order: insemination timing and semen handling; then subclinical endometritis by Metricheck or cytology; then energy balance and BHB; then heat stress; then infectious causes such as BVD, IBR, leptospirosis and neosporosis. Most repeat breeders in tropical smallholder herds are found in the first three."

**Q. "Can sensors detect these diseases?"**
"They detect **deviations** that precede them — a fall in rumination, a rise or fall in temperature, changes in activity and drinking — often before the animal looks sick. Rumen pH boluses genuinely change SARA diagnosis, because SARA is defined by time spent below a pH threshold and that is exactly what continuous measurement captures. But no sensor gives a diagnosis. It tells you which cow to walk to. Validate any system in your own herd before you scale it."
